# GDPR maximum fines and lawful processing: EU-wide overview as of 18 November 2026

**Currency limitation:** This overview explains the GDPR provisions and official guidance cited below. It does not confirm whether amendments or later judgments after 2024 affect every point through 18 November 2026. The final adoption status of possible newer EDPB legitimate-interest guidance has not been confirmed.

## 1. Core answer in brief

Article 83 establishes two main maximum administrative fine tiers:

- **EUR 10 million**, or, for an undertaking, **2% of its total worldwide annual turnover** of the preceding financial year, whichever is higher; and
- **EUR 20 million**, or, for an undertaking, **4% of its total worldwide annual turnover** of the preceding financial year, whichever is higher. [1, Article 83(4)–(6)]

These are **statutory ceilings**, not automatic penalties. A supervisory authority must decide whether to impose a fine and, if so, its amount, applying Article 83’s individual assessment factors. Each fine must be effective, proportionate and dissuasive. The CJEU has also held that an administrative fine requires an intentional or negligent infringement. [1, Article 83(1)–(2)][3][4]

For lawful processing, Article 6(1) provides **six lawful bases**: consent, contract necessity, legal obligation, vital interests, public task or official authority, and legitimate interests. A lawful basis is necessary, but **does not by itself establish full GDPR compliance**. Processing must also satisfy Article 5 and other applicable requirements, including the consent rules and the additional conditions for special-category and criminal-conviction data. [1]

## 2. GDPR administrative fines under Article 83

### 2.1 The two fine tiers

#### Lower tier: Article 83(4)

The maximum is **EUR 10 million**, or, for an undertaking, **2% of total worldwide annual turnover of the preceding financial year, whichever is higher**.

This tier covers infringements of:

- controller and processor obligations under **Articles 8, 11, 25–39, 42 and 43**;
- certification-body obligations under **Articles 42 and 43**; and
- monitoring-body obligations under **Article 41(4)**. [1, Article 83(4)]

Article 8’s specific conditions for children’s consent therefore appear in the lower tier, while the general lawfulness and consent provisions in Articles 6 and 7 appear in the upper tier.

#### Upper tier: Article 83(5)

The maximum is **EUR 20 million**, or, for an undertaking, **4% of total worldwide annual turnover of the preceding financial year, whichever is higher**.

Article 83(5) covers infringements of:

- the basic principles for processing, including conditions for consent, under **Articles 5, 6, 7 and 9**;
- data subject rights under **Articles 12–22**;
- transfers to a recipient in a third country or international organisation under **Articles 44–49**;
- obligations under Member State law adopted under **Chapter IX**; and
- non-compliance with a supervisory authority’s order, or temporary or definitive limitation on processing or suspension of data flows, under **Article 58(2)**, or failure to provide access in violation of **Article 58(1)**. [1, Article 83(5)]

#### Upper tier: Article 83(6)

Article 83(6) separately provides the same **EUR 20 million / 4%** ceiling for **non-compliance with an order by the supervisory authority under Article 58(2)**. It is therefore directed specifically at failure to comply with an authority’s order, rather than creating another general category of processing obligations. [1, Article 83(6)]

### 2.2 Which ceiling applies?

For an undertaking, the GDPR uses a **“whichever is higher”** rule: the applicable fixed euro amount is compared with the relevant percentage of total worldwide annual turnover. The higher amount is the legal ceiling. [1, Article 83(4)–(6)]

The relevant turnover is not necessarily confined to the legal entity directly responsible for the infringement. In **C‑807/21, Deutsche Wohnen**, the CJEU held that “undertaking” has the meaning used in EU competition law under Articles 101 and 102 TFEU. Where the responsible entity forms part of a single economic unit, the undertaking’s turnover can therefore include group turnover. This does not mean that every corporate group automatically constitutes one undertaking. [4]

The ceiling remains distinct from the actual fine. A 4% ceiling does not mean that an authority must impose a fine of 4%.

### 2.3 What “preceding financial year” means

Article 83 specifies **total worldwide annual turnover of the preceding financial year** as the percentage-ceiling benchmark. [1, Article 83(4)–(6)]

The statutory wording alone should not be treated as resolving every timing question, particularly where the infringement, investigation and fining decision occur in different years. The financial year used in a particular case should be checked against the applicable interpretation and decision. Nor should turnover be assumed to matter only to the ceiling: the actual fine must also satisfy effectiveness, proportionality and deterrence requirements. [1, Article 83(1)][2]

### 2.4 Linked processing and multiple infringements

Where a controller or processor intentionally or negligently infringes several GDPR provisions through the **same or linked processing operations**, Article 83(3) limits the total fine to the amount specified for the **gravest infringement**. Authorities cannot simply add together all statutory maxima for those operations. [1, Article 83(3)]

This rule does not mean that additional infringements must be ignored when assessing the case.

### 2.5 Public authorities and Member State discretion

Under **Article 83(7)**, Member States may determine whether and to what extent administrative fines can be imposed on public authorities and public bodies established in their territory. Public-sector fining exposure is therefore not uniform across the EU. [1]

Two further provisions address different matters:

- **Article 83(8)** requires appropriate procedural safeguards, including effective judicial remedy and due process.
- **Article 83(9)** addresses legal systems that do not provide for administrative fines. It permits implementation through initiation by the supervisory authority and imposition by competent national courts, subject to equivalent effectiveness and effective, proportionate and dissuasive penalties. It is not a second public-body exemption. [1]

### 2.6 Fine maximums vs actual fines

The distinction is:

- **Statutory maximum:** the highest amount permitted under the applicable ceiling.
- **Decision whether to fine:** the authority’s case-specific assessment, subject to the applicable legal requirements.
- **Actual fine:** the amount imposed after that assessment.

The GDPR does not require a fine for every infringement. An authority may use other corrective measures instead of, or alongside, a fine. Any fine imposed must be effective, proportionate and dissuasive. [1, Articles 58(2) and 83(1)–(2)]

## 3. How authorities decide whether a fine is imposed and how large it is

### 3.1 Article 83(2): mandatory assessment factors

Authorities must give due regard to:

- the **nature, gravity and duration** of the infringement, including the processing’s nature, scope or purpose, the number of people affected and the damage suffered;
- its **intentional or negligent character**;
- action taken to **mitigate damage**;
- the controller’s or processor’s **degree of responsibility**, taking account of measures under Articles 25 and 32;
- **relevant previous infringements**;
- cooperation with the authority to remedy the infringement and mitigate adverse effects;
- the **categories of personal data** affected;
- how the authority learned of the infringement, including whether and to what extent it was notified;
- compliance with earlier Article 58(2) measures concerning the same subject matter;
- adherence to approved **codes of conduct or certification mechanisms**; and
- other aggravating or mitigating factors, including financial benefits gained or losses avoided through the infringement. [1, Article 83(2)(a)–(k)]

### 3.2 The fine is not automatic

Article 83(2) requires an individual decision on whether a fine is appropriate and, if so, its amount. A breach therefore does not automatically require either a fine or the maximum fine. Other corrective measures may be imposed instead. [1]

### 3.3 Intentional or negligent conduct is required

In **C‑807/21, Deutsche Wohnen**, and **C‑683/21, Nacionalinis visuomenės sveikatos centras**, the CJEU held that an administrative fine may be imposed only where the infringement was committed **intentionally or negligently**. Fault is therefore more than a factor affecting the amount: it is a condition for imposing an Article 83 fine. [3][4]

C‑683/21 also addresses a controller’s exposure for processing carried out by a processor on its behalf. Such exposure can arise, but it does not extend without qualification to every act of a processor, including processing outside the relevant arrangement or instructions. [3]

These judgments interpret Article 83; they do not create a new fine tier.

### 3.4 The role of EDPB guidance

The EDPB’s **Guidelines 04/2022** address the calculation of administrative fines under Article 83. They should be read alongside the statutory assessment factors, applicable ceilings and CJEU interpretation. They do not replace the requirement for an individual assessment or create an automatic tariff. [1][2]

## 4. The six lawful bases for processing under Article 6

Article 6(1) provides a **closed list**: processing is lawful only if at least one of its six bases applies.

### 4.1 Consent — Article 6(1)(a)

Processing may be based on the data subject’s consent for one or more specific purposes. Consent must be:

- freely given;
- specific;
- informed;
- unambiguous; and
- expressed through a statement or clear affirmative action. [1, Articles 4(11) and 6(1)(a)]

The controller must be able to demonstrate consent. Silence, inactivity and pre-ticked boxes do not meet the standard. Withdrawal must be as easy as giving consent. [1, Article 7 and Recital 32][5]

An imbalance of power can make freely given consent unlikely, particularly in relationships involving public authorities or dependence. It is not, by itself, an absolute rule invalidating consent in every such relationship; genuine freedom of choice must be assessed. [1, Recital 43][5]

### 4.2 Contract necessity — Article 6(1)(b)

This basis covers processing necessary for:

- performance of a contract with the data subject; or
- steps taken at the data subject’s request before entering into a contract. [1]

The necessity test is narrow. Processing must be **objectively necessary**, not merely useful, convenient or included in contractual terms. The EDPB’s Article 6(1)(b) guidance addresses this issue in the context of online services. [6]

### 4.3 Legal obligation — Article 6(1)(c)

Processing must be necessary to comply with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject. Under **Article 6(3)**, the basis must be laid down in Union or Member State law. A private contractual obligation is not sufficient for this purpose. [1]

### 4.4 Vital interests — Article 6(1)(d)

Processing must be necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or another natural person. This is a narrow basis, typically relevant to protecting life or physical integrity, rather than ordinary commercial interests. [1, Article 6(1)(d) and Recital 46]

### 4.5 Public task / official authority — Article 6(1)(e)

Processing must be necessary for a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller. **Article 6(3)** requires the basis to be laid down in Union or Member State law. An organisation’s own view that an activity benefits the public is not enough. [1]

### 4.6 Legitimate interests — Article 6(1)(f)

This basis requires:

- a legitimate interest pursued by the controller or a third party;
- processing necessary for that interest; and
- an assessment of whether the data subject’s interests or fundamental rights and freedoms override it, particularly where the data subject is a child. [1]

The balancing assessment is fact-specific. **Public authorities cannot rely on Article 6(1)(f) for processing carried out in the performance of their tasks.** [1]

## 5. Why a lawful basis is not enough by itself

An Article 6 basis addresses one part of GDPR compliance. Processing must also satisfy other applicable obligations, including:

- transparency and fairness;
- purpose limitation;
- data minimisation;
- accuracy and retention limits;
- appropriate security;
- accountability;
- data subject rights;
- international transfer requirements; and
- additional conditions for special-category and criminal-conviction data. [1]

A controller can therefore have an Article 6 basis and still infringe the GDPR through the way it collects, uses, retains, secures or transfers personal data.

## 6. Article 5 principles that still apply even when a lawful basis exists

### 6.1 Lawfulness, fairness and transparency

Processing must be lawful, fair and transparent to the data subject. [1, Article 5(1)(a)]

### 6.2 Purpose limitation

Data must be collected for specified, explicit and legitimate purposes and not further processed incompatibly with those purposes. Further processing for public-interest archiving, scientific or historical research, or statistical purposes receives specific treatment subject to Article 89(1). [1, Article 5(1)(b)]

### 6.3 Data minimisation

Data must be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary for the purposes. [1, Article 5(1)(c)]

### 6.4 Accuracy

Data must be accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date. Reasonable steps must be taken to erase or rectify inaccurate data without delay, having regard to the processing purposes. [1, Article 5(1)(d)]

### 6.5 Storage limitation

Identifiable data must generally be retained no longer than necessary. Longer retention for specified archiving, research or statistical purposes is subject to the GDPR’s conditions and safeguards. [1, Article 5(1)(e)]

### 6.6 Integrity and confidentiality

Processing must ensure appropriate security, including protection against unauthorised or unlawful processing and accidental loss, destruction or damage. [1, Article 5(1)(f)]

### 6.7 Accountability

The controller is responsible for, and must be able to demonstrate compliance with, these principles. [1, Article 5(2)]

## 7. Article 7 consent requirements

Where consent is used:

- the controller must be able to **demonstrate valid consent**;
- a consent request within a written declaration addressing other matters must be clearly distinguishable, intelligible, accessible and expressed in clear and plain language;
- the data subject must be informed of the right to withdraw before giving consent;
- withdrawal must be possible at any time and as easy as giving consent; and
- assessing whether consent is freely given requires particular attention to whether a contract or service is conditional on consent to unnecessary processing. [1, Article 7]

Withdrawal does not affect the lawfulness of consent-based processing before withdrawal. The EDPB guidance also explains the importance of separate choices for distinct purposes rather than undifferentiated bundled consent. [1, Article 7(3)][5]

**Children’s consent:** Where a consent-based information society service is offered directly to a child, Article 8 sets a default age of **16** for the child’s own consent. Member States may lower that age, but not below **13**. Below the applicable age, consent must be given or authorised by the holder of parental responsibility, and the controller must make reasonable verification efforts, taking available technology into account. Article 8 is not a general rule for every processing activity involving children. [1, Article 8]

## 8. Special-category data under Article 9

### 8.1 The general rule

Article 9(1) generally prohibits processing data revealing:

- racial or ethnic origin;
- political opinions;
- religious or philosophical beliefs;
- trade union membership;
- genetic data;
- biometric data used to uniquely identify a person;
- health data; or
- data concerning sex life or sexual orientation. [1]

### 8.2 The exceptions in Article 9(2)

The ten exceptions are:

1. **Explicit consent**, unless Union or Member State law prevents the prohibition being lifted by consent.
2. Necessary processing for **employment, social security or social protection obligations and rights**, where authorised by law or a qualifying collective agreement with appropriate safeguards.
3. Protection of **vital interests** where the person is physically or legally incapable of consenting.
4. Specified legitimate activities of a **not-for-profit body** with political, philosophical, religious or trade-union aims, subject to membership/contact limits, safeguards and restrictions on external disclosure.
5. Data **manifestly made public** by the data subject.
6. Necessary processing for **legal claims**, or courts acting in their judicial capacity.
7. Necessary processing for **substantial public interest**, based on Union or Member State law meeting proportionality and safeguard requirements.
8. Necessary processing for specified **medical, health or social-care purposes**, based on law or a contract with a health professional and subject to Article 9(3)’s confidentiality conditions.
9. Necessary processing for **public interest in public health**, based on Union or Member State law with suitable and specific safeguards.
10. Necessary processing for **public-interest archiving, scientific or historical research, or statistical purposes**, based on Union or Member State law, subject to Article 89(1), proportionality and suitable safeguards. [1, Article 9(2)]

### 8.3 Important limitations

Special-category processing requires both an **Article 6 lawful basis** and an applicable **Article 9 exception**. Notice or transparency alone cannot replace either requirement. Each exception has its own conditions; they are not interchangeable permissions. [1]

### 8.4 Member State law issues

Several exceptions depend on Union or Member State law. Health and social-care processing can also rely on a qualifying contract with a health professional, subject to confidentiality conditions.

Under **Article 9(4)**, Member States may maintain or introduce further conditions, including limitations, for genetic, biometric and health data. National law may therefore materially affect the available route. [1]

## 9. Criminal-conviction and offence data under Article 10

### 9.1 Main rule

Processing personal data relating to criminal convictions, offences or related security measures, on the basis of Article 6(1), is permitted only:

- under the control of official authority; or
- where authorised by Union or Member State law providing appropriate safeguards. [1, Article 10]

A **comprehensive register of criminal convictions** may be kept only under the control of official authority. [1]

### 9.2 Why Article 10 matters

Article 10 is a separate heightened-protection rule, not another Article 9 exception. An Article 6 basis alone is insufficient, and Member State law can be decisive. [1]

Article 10 is **not expressly listed** in Article 83(4) or (5). Its infringement should therefore not automatically be assigned to a fine tier merely by analogy with Article 9. Any administrative-fine analysis requires attention to the provisions actually infringed and other applicable enforcement rules. [1]

## 10. Practical reading of the GDPR’s lawfulness structure

A sound analysis asks:

1. **What purposes and processing operations are involved?**
2. **Which Article 6 basis applies?**
3. **Are the Article 5 principles and accountability requirements satisfied?**
4. **If consent is used, are Articles 4(11) and 7 satisfied, and does Article 8 apply?**
5. **For special-category data, which Article 9 exception applies and what conditions accompany it?**
6. **For criminal-conviction or offence data, does Article 10 permit the processing?**
7. **Are other applicable obligations met, including rights, security and transfer requirements?**
8. **If an infringement occurs, which corrective powers and fine provisions apply?** [1]

## 11. Important EU-wide caveats

An EU-wide overview cannot replace a Member State and fact-specific assessment:

- **Public-authority fines** depend partly on national choices under Article 83(7).
- **Children’s consent ages** can vary between 13 and 16 under Article 8.
- Several **Article 9 and Article 10 permissions** depend on national law.
- Additional national conditions may apply to **genetic, biometric and health data**.
- Necessity under Article 6 and the legitimate-interest balancing assessment depend on the actual processing.
- Consent validity depends on genuine choice, information, purpose specificity and the processing relationship. [1][5][6]

The final status of possible newer EDPB legitimate-interest guidance has not been confirmed. No conclusion here depends on treating a possible 2026 version as finally adopted or as changing Article 6(1)(f).

## 12. Bottom line

The GDPR’s maximum administrative fine is a **two-tier ceiling system**, not a single automatic penalty:

- **EUR 10 million**, or for an undertaking **2% of worldwide annual turnover**, whichever is higher; and
- **EUR 20 million**, or for an undertaking **4% of worldwide annual turnover**, whichever is higher.

The turnover reference is the **preceding financial year**. The relevant undertaking may extend beyond the directly responsible legal entity. A fine requires intentional or negligent infringement, and its amount must reflect Article 83’s individual assessment and effectiveness, proportionality and deterrence requirements. [1][3][4]

For lawful processing, one of the **six Article 6 bases** is necessary but not sufficient. Article 5 principles, consent conditions, Article 9 exceptions, Article 10 restrictions and other applicable GDPR obligations must also be satisfied. National law and the particular facts remain decisive in several areas. [1]
