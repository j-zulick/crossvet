# The Paris Agreement’s temperature goals and NDC update rules

## 1) What the Paris Agreement set as the long-term temperature goal

The Paris Agreement’s central temperature aim is stated in Article 2.1(a): Parties agreed to hold the increase in global average temperature to **well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels** and to **pursue efforts to limit the temperature increase to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels**. This is the Agreement’s long-term mitigation objective and the core benchmark for assessing collective climate action. The same article also ties this temperature goal to strengthening the global response to climate change, including by improving adaptation capacity and aligning finance flows with low-emissions, climate-resilient development. [1]

In practical terms, the wording matters:

- **“Well below 2°C”** sets a stricter ceiling than a simple “below 2°C” target.
- **“Pursue efforts to limit to 1.5°C”** does not make 1.5°C a separate guaranteed outcome, but it clearly elevates it as an additional ambition level that Parties should strive toward.
- The Agreement does **not** assign the temperature goal to any single country’s NDC; rather, it establishes a **collective global goal** that successive national contributions are meant to support. [1]

## 2) How Article 4 structures successive NDCs

Article 4 is the main provision governing nationally determined contributions (NDCs). It creates a continuing cycle of preparation, communication, maintenance, and improvement of national climate pledges. [1]

### 2.1 The basic obligation: successive NDCs

Under Article 4.2, each Party shall **prepare, communicate and maintain successive nationally determined contributions** that it intends to achieve. Parties must also pursue domestic mitigation measures, with the aim of achieving the objectives of those contributions. This makes NDCs an ongoing process rather than a one-time filing. [1]

### 2.2 Progression and highest possible ambition

Article 4.3 sets two key expectations for each successive NDC:

- it must represent a **progression** beyond the Party’s previous NDC; and
- it must reflect the Party’s **highest possible ambition**, taking into account its common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, in light of different national circumstances. [1]

This is the Agreement’s core “ratchet” logic. It does not require every NDC to be identical in format or magnitude, but it does require that each new contribution move forward rather than stand still or step backward. The ambition standard is framed in a nationally differentiated way, but always under a progression requirement. [1]

### 2.3 Updating an NDC is possible at any time

Article 4.11 provides that a Party **may at any time adjust its existing NDC with a view to enhancing its level of ambition**. This means countries are not locked into waiting for the next regular cycle if they decide to strengthen their target sooner. The provision points in one direction only: adjustments are for **enhancement**, not for lowering ambition. [1]

## 3) The five-year cycle for communicating NDCs

The Agreement establishes a **five-year communication cycle** for NDCs in Article 4.9. Each Party shall communicate a new NDC **every five years**. [1]

This five-year cycle is one of the Paris Agreement’s most important design features. It creates regular moments when countries revisit their targets, compare them with the global temperature goals, and ideally strengthen them over time. The point is not merely administrative timing; it is to create recurring opportunities for higher ambition. [1]

A useful way to distinguish the rules is:

- **Article 4.9**: the regular five-year obligation to communicate an NDC.
- **Article 4.11**: the option to revise and enhance an NDC at any time between those five-year points. [1]

So, the Agreement combines:
1. a **mandatory five-year submission rhythm**, and  
2. a **flexible upgrade mechanism** whenever a Party is ready to increase ambition. [1]

## 4) Timing and submission expectations under UNFCCC/COP guidance

The Paris Agreement itself sets the five-year cycle, but the detailed timing of when a new NDC should be submitted relative to the relevant meeting was further clarified in COP decision text. Decision 1/CP.21, paragraph 25, requested that Parties whose NDCs contained a time frame up to 2025 communicate a new NDC by 2020, and that all Parties communicate new or updated NDCs **at least 9 to 12 months in advance of the relevant session of the Conference of the Parties serving as the meeting of the Parties to the Paris Agreement (CMA)**, to facilitate clarity, transparency, and understanding. [2]

The practical effect is that NDCs are expected to arrive early enough for:
- public review,
- international understanding of each Party’s intent, and
- collective assessment ahead of the formal meeting cycle. [2]

UNFCCC guidance pages on NDCs also describe the Paris system as a five-year cycle in which Parties can update or enhance ambition as needed, reinforcing the treaty text’s ratchet design. [3]

## 5) How the global stocktake feeds into later NDCs

Article 14 establishes the **global stocktake**. This is a collective process, not a country-by-country compliance hearing. Its purpose is to assess collective progress toward the Agreement’s purpose and its long-term goals, including the temperature goal, adaptation, and support. [1]

### 5.1 What the stocktake assesses

Article 14 says the stocktake shall occur **in light of equity and the best available science** and assess collective progress toward achieving the purpose of the Agreement and its long-term goals. That means it is meant to measure how the world is doing overall, not to judge individual targets in isolation. [1]

### 5.2 When it happens

The Agreement provides that the global stocktake shall take place **every five years**, starting in **2023**, unless the Conference of the Parties serving as the meeting of the Parties to the Paris Agreement decides otherwise. [1]

### 5.3 Why it matters for future NDCs

Article 14.3 states that the outcome of the global stocktake **shall inform Parties in updating and enhancing their actions and support** in accordance with the relevant provisions of the Agreement, and in enhancing international cooperation for climate action. [1]

This is a key part of the Paris architecture:

1. Parties submit NDCs on a five-year cycle.
2. The global stocktake evaluates collective progress.
3. The stocktake outcome then helps shape the next round of NDCs, encouraging stronger action and better support. [1]

In short, the stocktake is the feedback loop that is supposed to push future NDCs upward in ambition.

## 6) How the Paris system fits together

The Agreement’s design can be understood as a cycle:

- **Temperature goal**: keep warming well below 2°C and pursue 1.5°C. [1]
- **NDCs**: each Party prepares and maintains successive contributions. [1]
- **Progression**: each new NDC must go beyond the previous one and represent the Party’s highest possible ambition. [1]
- **Five-year cadence**: every Party communicates a new NDC every five years. [1]
- **Early submission guidance**: NDCs should be communicated well ahead of the relevant CMA session to aid clarity and transparency. [2]
- **Global stocktake**: every five years, Parties collectively assess progress; the result informs later NDCs. [1]

This structure is often described as a “ratchet mechanism” because it is designed to repeatedly tighten ambition over time without requiring a complete treaty overhaul.

## 7) What the Agreement does and does not require

### 7.1 What it clearly requires

The Paris Agreement clearly requires:
- a long-term temperature objective;
- successive NDCs;
- five-year communication;
- progression beyond the previous NDC;
- highest possible ambition;
- the possibility of strengthening an NDC at any time; and
- a periodic global stocktake that informs later action. [1][2]

### 7.2 What it does not do

The Agreement does **not** prescribe one uniform emissions target for all countries, and it does **not** require every NDC to be legally identical in scope or format. It also does not guarantee that the temperature goals will be met; rather, it establishes a system intended to move collective ambition in that direction over time. [1]

## 8) Bottom line

The Paris Agreement’s long-term temperature goal is to keep warming **well below 2°C** while **pursuing efforts to limit warming to 1.5°C**. To move toward that goal, Article 4 creates a five-year NDC cycle, requires each new NDC to show **progression** and **highest possible ambition**, and allows Parties to strengthen their pledges at any time. Article 14 adds the global stocktake, a five-year collective review that is intended to inform subsequent NDCs and increase ambition over time. [1][2][3]
