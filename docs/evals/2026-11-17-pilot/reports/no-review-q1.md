# GDPR maximum fines and lawful processing of personal data

## Executive summary

Under the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the maximum administrative fine is not a single amount. Article 83 creates two main fine tiers, each expressed as the higher of a fixed euro amount or a percentage of the undertaking’s total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year. The lower tier is up to €10 million or 2%; the higher tier is up to €20 million or 4%. Which tier applies depends on the type of infringement. [1]

For processing personal data to be lawful, a controller must satisfy **at least one** of the six lawful bases in Article 6, **and** comply with the GDPR’s broader principles in Article 5. A lawful basis does not by itself make processing compliant if, for example, the processing is unfair, excessive, insecure, or lacks a valid purpose limitation. [1][2]

Special-category data under Article 9 and criminal-conviction/offence data under Article 10 have extra restrictions. In practice, Article 9 data are prohibited unless a specific exception applies, and Article 10 data may be processed only under official authority control or where Union law or Member State law provides appropriate safeguards. [1]

The GDPR text itself is the primary source, supplemented by EDPB guidance on consent, legitimate interests, and administrative fines. [1][2][3][4]

## 1) Article 83 GDPR: the administrative fine tiers

### 1.1 The two maximum fine tiers

Article 83 sets two infringement bands:

- **Tier 1**: up to **€10,000,000** or, in the case of an undertaking, **up to 2% of total worldwide annual turnover** for the preceding financial year, whichever is higher. [1]
- **Tier 2**: up to **€20,000,000** or, in the case of an undertaking, **up to 4% of total worldwide annual turnover** for the preceding financial year, whichever is higher. [1]

The “higher of” rule means the authority compares the fixed euro ceiling and the turnover percentage, and the maximum applicable cap is the larger figure. [1][3]

### 1.2 How to choose the applicable maximum

The applicable tier is determined by the **kind of infringement**:

- If the infringement falls in Article 83(4), the maximum is the **Tier 1** cap. [1]
- If the infringement falls in Article 83(5) or Article 83(6), the maximum is the **Tier 2** cap. [1]

Article 83(3) adds an important limit: where the same processing operations or linked operations involve multiple infringements, the total administrative fine must not exceed the amount specified for the most serious infringement. In other words, fines are not simply added up without limit for connected breaches. [1][3]

### 1.3 What “undertaking” means for turnover-based caps

The percentage cap is based on the undertaking’s total worldwide annual turnover. The EDPB explains that “undertaking” should be understood in the EU competition-law sense, which may include a group of companies as one economic unit where control and economic reality justify that approach. This matters because the relevant turnover can be broader than the single legal entity that committed the breach. [3]

### 1.4 Article 83(4): lower-tier infringements

Article 83(4) covers infringements of:

- Article 8
- Article 11
- Articles 25 to 39
- Articles 42 and 43

These are the main subject areas:

- **Article 8**: conditions for children’s consent in relation to information society services. [1]
- **Article 11**: processing where identification is not required. [1]
- **Articles 25–39**: core operational and governance duties, including data protection by design and by default, joint controllers, records, cooperation, security-related duties, notification duties, data protection impact assessments, prior consultation, DPO requirements, codes of conduct, and certification structures. [1]
- **Articles 42 and 43**: certification mechanisms and certification bodies. [1]

### 1.5 Article 83(5): higher-tier infringements

Article 83(5) covers infringements of:

- the **basic principles for processing**, including the conditions for consent, under **Articles 5, 6, 7 and 9**
- the **data subjects’ rights** under **Articles 12 to 22**
- transfers of personal data to a recipient in a third country or international organisation under **Articles 44 to 49**
- any obligations pursuant to Member State law adopted under **Chapter IX**
- non-compliance with an order by a supervisory authority under **Article 58(2)** [1]

This is the higher tier because these breaches go to the core legality of processing, rights of individuals, international transfers, and compliance with binding supervisory measures. [1][3]

### 1.6 Article 83(6): failure to comply with a supervisory order

Article 83(6) specifically covers failure to comply with an order by a supervisory authority under Article 58(2). The effect is to place non-compliance with a binding corrective order in the highest fine tier. [1]

### 1.7 Article 83(1) and 83(2): fines are not automatic

The GDPR does **not** say the maximum must be imposed. Article 83(1) requires fines to be effective, proportionate, and dissuasive. Article 83(2) requires the authority to consider all relevant circumstances, including:

- nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement
- number of affected data subjects and level of damage
- whether the infringement was intentional or negligent
- actions taken to mitigate damage
- degree of responsibility
- prior infringements
- cooperation with the authority
- categories of personal data affected
- how the authority became aware of the infringement
- compliance with earlier measures
- adherence to approved codes of conduct or certification
- any other aggravating or mitigating factor [1][3]

So the legal maximum is a ceiling, not a default penalty. [1][3]

### 1.8 Member State qualifications affecting fines

There are important national-law qualifications:

- **Article 83(7)** allows Member States to determine whether, and to what extent, administrative fines may be imposed on public authorities and bodies. This means the GDPR does not require identical fining regimes for public-sector bodies across all Member States. [1]
- **Article 83(8)** requires appropriate procedural safeguards under Union law and Member State law, including effective judicial remedy and due process protections. [1]

Also, some GDPR breaches may be subject to additional or alternative national sanctions under **Article 84**, where Member States create rules for infringements not already covered by Article 83. [1]

## 2) The six lawful bases for processing under Article 6

Article 6(1) lists six lawful bases. Processing is lawful only if at least one applies. [1][2]

### 2.1 Consent — Article 6(1)(a)

Processing is lawful if the data subject has given consent for one or more specific purposes.

Key requirements:

- Consent must be **freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous**. [1][4]
- It must be demonstrated by a clear affirmative act; silence, pre-ticked boxes, or inactivity do not count. [1][4]
- Consent can be withdrawn at any time, and withdrawal must be as easy as giving consent. Withdrawal does not affect prior lawful processing based on consent before withdrawal. [1][4]
- Consent is not freely given if there is a clear imbalance of power, or if consent is bundled with something unnecessary for the service in a way that makes it non-voluntary. [1][4]
- Consent must be granular where multiple purposes are involved; a bundled “take it or leave it” request is often invalid unless all processing is genuinely necessary for the service or purpose. [1][4]

Additional child-specific rule:
- For information society services offered directly to a child, Article 8 requires parental authorization if the child is below the Member State’s chosen age threshold between 13 and 16 years. [1]

### 2.2 Contract necessity — Article 6(1)(b)

Processing is lawful if it is necessary for:

- the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party, or
- steps at the request of the data subject before entering into a contract. [1]

Key limits:

- The processing must be objectively necessary for the contract or pre-contract request; convenience is not enough. [2]
- The contract basis cannot be used to justify processing that is merely useful, ancillary, or added for the controller’s own benefit unless it is truly necessary for the contract. [2]
- The data subject must be a party to the contract, or the processing must relate to steps taken at the data subject’s request before the contract. [1]

### 2.3 Legal obligation — Article 6(1)(c)

Processing is lawful if it is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject. [1]

Key limits:

- The obligation must arise under Union law or Member State law that applies to the controller. [1]
- The law must be sufficiently clear about the processing purpose and scope; Article 6(3) requires the legal basis to determine the purpose of processing and, where relevant, contain specific provisions on the type of data, purpose, storage, access, and related safeguards. [1]
- The controller cannot rely on this basis for processing that goes beyond what the legal obligation requires. [2]

### 2.4 Vital interests — Article 6(1)(d)

Processing is lawful if it is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or another natural person. [1]

Key limits:

- This is generally reserved for situations involving life-or-death or similarly serious emergencies.
- It is usually used when no other lawful basis is practically available in an urgent situation. [2]

### 2.5 Public task / official authority — Article 6(1)(e)

Processing is lawful if it is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller. [1]

Key limits:

- The public-interest task or official authority must have a basis in Union or Member State law under Article 6(3). [1]
- The controller must show that the processing is necessary for that task. [2]
- This basis is generally tied to public-law functions and is not simply a generic “public benefit” concept. [2]
- Where legitimate interests under Article 6(1)(f) are available, Article 6(1)(e) is the more appropriate basis for public-interest or official-authority processing. [1][2]

### 2.6 Legitimate interests — Article 6(1)(f)

Processing is lawful if it is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject, especially where the data subject is a child. [1]

Key limits:

- A **three-part test** is required in practice:
  1. identify a legitimate interest;
  2. show the processing is necessary for that interest;
  3. balance that interest against the data subject’s rights and freedoms. [2][3]
- The interests must be genuine, lawful, and sufficiently specific. [2][3]
- Necessity means the processing must be reasonably needed; if a less intrusive means can achieve the purpose, the necessity test may fail. [2][3]
- The balancing step must consider the context, the reasonable expectations of the data subject, the nature of the data, the impact of the processing, and any safeguards. [2][3]
- Legitimate interests may not be used by public authorities in the performance of their tasks. [1][3]
- Children deserve particular protection in the balancing exercise. [1][3]

### 2.7 Articles 6(2) and 6(3): national law and specification

Article 6(2) and 6(3) are especially important for the legal obligation and public task bases:

- Article 6(2) preserves or allows Member State rules for more specific processing requirements.
- Article 6(3) requires that the legal basis for processing under Article 6(1)(c) or (e) be set out in Union or Member State law and that the purpose of processing be determined by that law or be necessary for a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority. [1]

This means that a valid Article 6 basis may depend on a separate legal instrument, not just the GDPR itself. [1]

### 2.8 Article 6(4): change of purpose

If processing is for a purpose other than the one for which the data were initially collected, Article 6(4) requires a compatibility assessment unless the new purpose is based on consent or Union/Member State law.

Relevant factors include:

- any link between the original and new purposes
- the context in which the data were collected
- the nature of the data, especially whether special-category data are involved
- the possible consequences for data subjects
- the existence of appropriate safeguards [1]

This matters because a valid lawful basis for the original collection does not automatically justify later reuse for a different purpose. [1]

## 3) Article 5: lawful basis is not the same as compliance with the processing principles

Article 6 tells you whether processing has a lawful basis. Article 5 tells you how all processing must be carried out. The two are distinct and cumulative. [1]

### 3.1 The Article 5 principles

Processing must be:

- **lawful, fair, and transparent**
- collected for **specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes**
- **adequate, relevant, and limited** to what is necessary
- **accurate** and kept up to date where needed
- kept in a form permitting identification no longer than necessary
- processed with appropriate **integrity and confidentiality**
- performed under the **accountability** principle, meaning the controller must be able to demonstrate compliance [1]

### 3.2 Why a lawful basis is not enough

A controller may have an Article 6 basis and still violate Article 5. For example:

- consent may be invalid if it is not freely given or not properly informed;
- a legitimate-interest basis may fail if the processing is disproportionate;
- processing may be lawful in theory but still unfair, excessive, or improperly retained. [1][2][3][4]

So lawful processing requires both a valid Article 6 basis and compliance with Article 5. [1]

## 4) Consent under Article 7 and the GDPR’s consent standard

Article 7 sets the operational rules for consent when consent is relied on as the legal basis. [1][4]

### 4.1 Conditions for valid consent

Consent must be:

- **freely given**
- **specific**
- **informed**
- **unambiguous** [1][4]

### 4.2 Demonstrability

The controller must be able to show that consent was given. In practice, this means records, logs, or other evidence are needed. [1][4]

### 4.3 Withdrawal

The data subject must be able to withdraw consent at any time, and it must be as easy to withdraw as it was to give consent. [1][4]

### 4.4 Freely given consent and power imbalance

Consent is not valid if the data subject has no real choice. The EDPB stresses that consent is often invalid where there is a clear imbalance of power, because the data subject may feel unable to refuse. [4]

### 4.5 Specificity and granularity

Consent must be tied to specific purposes. A general “I agree to everything” request is not enough if different purposes are mixed together without real choice. [1][4]

### 4.6 No bundling where unnecessary

If consent is bundled with terms that are not necessary for the service, it may not be freely given. Consent should not be conditioned on unnecessary processing where the data subject cannot realistically refuse without losing something unrelated to the processing. [1][4]

## 5) Special-category data under Article 9

### 5.1 General rule: prohibition

Article 9(1) prohibits processing of personal data revealing:

- racial or ethnic origin
- political opinions
- religious or philosophical beliefs
- trade union membership
- genetic data
- biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person
- data concerning health
- data concerning a natural person’s sex life or sexual orientation [1]

### 5.2 The extra step required

Even if an Article 6 lawful basis exists, special-category data cannot be processed unless one of the Article 9(2) exceptions applies. So Article 6 is necessary, but not sufficient. [1]

### 5.3 The Article 9(2) exceptions

Processing may be allowed, among others, where:

- the data subject has given **explicit consent** for one or more specified purposes;
- processing is necessary for obligations and rights in employment, social security, and social protection law;
- processing is necessary to protect vital interests where the data subject is incapable of giving consent;
- processing is carried out in the course of legitimate activities by certain not-for-profit bodies with appropriate safeguards;
- the data have been manifestly made public by the data subject;
- processing is necessary for legal claims or courts acting in their judicial capacity;
- processing is necessary for substantial public interest on the basis of Union or Member State law with suitable safeguards;
- processing is necessary for preventive or occupational medicine, medical diagnosis, health or social care, or management of health or social care systems on the basis of Union or Member State law or contract with a health professional and subject to professional secrecy;
- processing is necessary for public interest in public health, again on the basis of Union or Member State law and appropriate safeguards;
- processing is necessary for archiving in the public interest, scientific or historical research, or statistical purposes, subject to safeguards. [1]

### 5.4 Explicit consent

Where consent is used for Article 9 data, it must be **explicit**, which is stricter than ordinary Article 6 consent. [1][4]

### 5.5 Article 9(4): Member State latitude

Member States may maintain or introduce further conditions, including limitations, with regard to genetic, biometric, or health data. This means national law may add extra restrictions beyond the GDPR baseline. [1]

## 6) Criminal-conviction and offence data under Article 10

Article 10 provides a separate regime for criminal-conviction and offence data. Processing may occur only:

- under the control of official authority, or
- where the processing is authorised by Union law or Member State law providing appropriate safeguards for the rights and freedoms of data subjects. [1]

Key points:

- This is not an ordinary Article 6 basis. Article 10 adds a further legal condition. [1]
- The law authorising processing must include safeguards. [1]
- Processing of criminal data is therefore narrower and more tightly controlled than ordinary personal data processing. [1]

## 7) Practical relationship among Articles 5, 6, 7, 9, and 10

A simple way to read the GDPR framework is:

1. **Article 5**: the processing must respect the general principles.  
2. **Article 6**: one lawful basis must apply.  
3. **Article 7**: if consent is used, it must meet the consent rules.  
4. **Article 9**: if special-category data are involved, an Article 9 exception must also apply.  
5. **Article 10**: if criminal-conviction/offence data are involved, the processing must be under official authority control or specifically authorised by law with safeguards. [1][2][4]

All layers may apply at once. For example, processing can be lawful under Article 6 but still unlawful if it lacks a valid Article 9 exception or violates Article 5’s principles. [1]

## 8) Material qualifications and caveats

### 8.1 No single Member State is assumed

The GDPR itself is directly applicable EU law, but it leaves room for Member State rules in several places:

- Article 6(2) and 6(3) for legal obligation and public task bases
- Article 8 for children’s consent age thresholds
- Article 9(4) for genetic, biometric, and health data
- Article 83(7) for public authorities and fines
- Chapter IX for national-law derogations and specifications [1]

So the final legality question can depend on national implementing law, even though the GDPR provides the baseline. [1]

### 8.2 “Lawful” does not mean “safe from enforcement”

Even where processing is believed to be lawful, a supervisory authority may still investigate whether the controller has correctly identified the legal basis, documented the balancing test, honored withdrawal of consent, used appropriate safeguards, or respected special-category restrictions. [1][3][4]

### 8.3 Maximum fine is not the likely fine

The Article 83 maxima are legal ceilings. In practice, the actual amount depends on the circumstances under Article 83(2), the seriousness of the breach, cooperation, and proportionality. [1][3]

## Conclusion

As of 18 November 2026, the GDPR’s maximum administrative fines remain structured in two tiers:

- **€10 million or 2% of worldwide annual turnover** for Article 83(4) infringements;
- **€20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover** for Article 83(5) and 83(6) infringements. [1]

For lawful processing, a controller must identify one of the six Article 6 bases and also comply with the broader Article 5 principles. If consent is used, Article 7’s requirements apply. If special-category data are involved, Article 9 adds a separate prohibition plus exceptions; if criminal-conviction or offence data are involved, Article 10 adds another layer of control and legal authorization. [1][2][4]

The main practical lesson is that GDPR compliance is cumulative: a lawful basis is necessary, but not enough on its own. [1]
