# The Federal Reserve’s Dual Mandate: What It Requires and Which Law Established It

## Bottom line

The Federal Reserve’s “dual mandate” is the statutory instruction to pursue **maximum employment** and **stable prices**. The relevant law that established this mandate was the **Federal Reserve Reform Act of 1977**, enacted as **Public Law 95-188** on **November 16, 1977**. That law added **Section 2A** to the Federal Reserve Act, now codified at **12 U.S.C. § 225a**. A later law, the **Full Employment and Balanced Growth Act of 1978** (the **Humphrey-Hawkins Act**), did **not** create the dual mandate; instead, it supplemented the framework by adding reporting and economic-policy requirements. [1][2][3][4][5]

## What the dual mandate requires

### The two core objectives

The Federal Reserve explains that its monetary policy is directed toward two statutory goals:

- **Maximum employment**
- **Stable prices**

The Fed’s own FAQ states that the Federal Reserve “conducts the nation’s monetary policy to promote the goals of maximum employment and stable prices” and that these are the goals Congress assigned in the Federal Reserve Act. The Board also explains that maximum employment is a broad, inclusive objective rather than a fixed numerical target, while stable prices means low and predictable inflation that supports economic decision-making. [1][2][3]

### How the Fed interprets “maximum employment”

The term **maximum employment** does **not** mean a permanently fixed unemployment rate or a single numerical target. The Federal Reserve describes it as the highest level of employment consistent with a stable inflation environment and sustainable economic conditions. Because labor markets change over time due to demographics, productivity, and other structural factors, the “maximum” level is not a fixed number that can be legislated in advance. [1][2][3]

### How the Fed interprets “stable prices”

The phrase **stable prices** refers to preserving the purchasing power of money by keeping inflation low and predictable. The statute itself does not specify a numeric inflation target. The Federal Reserve has chosen, as a policy objective, to aim for **2 percent inflation over the longer run**, but that 2 percent figure is a **Federal Reserve policy goal**, not the statutory text of the dual mandate. [1][2][3]

## The legal basis: Section 2A of the Federal Reserve Act

### The operative statutory provision

The dual mandate is found in **Section 2A of the Federal Reserve Act**, codified at **12 U.S.C. § 225a**. The current statutory language directs the Federal Reserve to conduct monetary policy so as to:

- “promote effectively the goals of **maximum employment**, **stable prices**, and **moderate long-term interest rates**.” [5]

That phrase is important because it is the source of the common shorthand “dual mandate.”

### Why it is called a “dual” mandate if the statute mentions three goals

The law refers to **three** objectives in Section 2A:

1. Maximum employment  
2. Stable prices  
3. Moderate long-term interest rates  

The term **dual mandate** is used because the first two—maximum employment and stable prices—are treated as the **core goals**. The third objective, **moderate long-term interest rates**, is generally understood as a **result** or **byproduct** of achieving the first two, rather than an independent policy target with equal status. In other words, the Federal Reserve does not set a separate long-term interest-rate target under the mandate; instead, moderate long-term rates are expected to follow from well-managed inflation and employment conditions. This is consistent with the Federal Reserve’s own explanations of its monetary-policy goals. [1][2][3][5]

## Which law established the mandate

### The establishing law: Federal Reserve Reform Act of 1977

The mandate was established by the **Federal Reserve Reform Act of 1977**, enacted as **Public Law 95-188** on **November 16, 1977**. The relevant provision is **Section 202**, which amended the Federal Reserve Act by inserting **a new Section 2A**. The enacted law appears in the Statutes at Large as **91 Stat. 1387**. [4][5]

### What Section 202 did

Section 202 of Public Law 95-188 inserted the modern monetary-policy objectives into the Federal Reserve Act. The enacted text added a duty for the Board of Governors and the Federal Open Market Committee to maintain long-run growth of monetary and credit aggregates consistent with the economy’s long-run potential, while also pursuing the goals that are now recognized as the dual mandate. [5]

## Distinguishing the 1977 establishing law from the 1978 supplement

### The 1977 law created the mandate

The key point is that the **1977 Federal Reserve Reform Act** is the legislation that **established** the mandate in Section 2A. That is the foundational legal source for the dual mandate framework. [4][5]

### The 1978 Humphrey-Hawkins Act supplemented the framework

The **Full Employment and Balanced Growth Act of 1978**—commonly called the **Humphrey-Hawkins Act**—came later and **did not create the dual mandate**. Instead, it expanded Congress’s broader employment-and-growth policy framework and added reporting and consultation requirements. Federal Reserve History describes the 1978 law as a supplement to the policy environment, not the source of the original mandate. [4][5]

### Why this distinction matters

This distinction is often confused because Humphrey-Hawkins became closely associated with Federal Reserve accountability and with the phrase “full employment.” But the legal command that the Federal Reserve conduct monetary policy toward maximum employment and stable prices traces to the **1977 amendment to the Federal Reserve Act**, not the 1978 Humphrey-Hawkins legislation. [4][5]

## Practical meaning of the statutory reference to moderate long-term interest rates

### How the third objective fits the mandate

The statute’s reference to **moderate long-term interest rates** is best understood as a consequence of the first two goals. Stable prices reduce inflation risk premia, and maximum employment supports durable growth, both of which tend to contribute to more moderate long-term rates over time. The Federal Reserve’s policy framework therefore treats this third phrase as part of the statutory statement of aims, but not as a separate, independently managed target. [1][2][3][5]

### Why the term “dual mandate” persists

Even though the statute mentions three objectives, the phrase **dual mandate** persists because:

- the first two goals are the core policy objectives;
- the third goal is commonly read as dependent on the first two;
- the Federal Reserve’s own public explanations emphasize the two primary goals. [1][2][3]

## Key takeaways

- The Federal Reserve’s dual mandate means pursuing **maximum employment** and **stable prices**. [1][2][3]
- The relevant statutory provision is **Section 2A of the Federal Reserve Act**, codified at **12 U.S.C. § 225a**. [5]
- The mandate was established by the **Federal Reserve Reform Act of 1977**, **Public Law 95-188**, enacted **November 16, 1977**. [4][5]
- The statute also refers to **moderate long-term interest rates**, but that phrase does not change the fact that the mandate is commonly called “dual” because the first two objectives are the central ones. [1][2][3][5]
- The **Humphrey-Hawkins Act of 1978** supplemented the framework with additional reporting and policy requirements; it was **not** the law that originally created the dual mandate. [4][5]
